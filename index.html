1. Introduction – Types of Solids

Matter exists in solid or fluid state. All materials are composed of atoms and molecules. A solid is essentially an ordered array of atoms bound together by electric forces.

Crystalline Regular, periodic arrangement extending throughout the sample → long-range order
Polycrystalline Many small crystals (grains) of irregular size separated by grain boundaries
Amorphous No long-range order → only short-range order (super-cooled liquids)
Schematic comparison of atomic arrangement in the three types of solids

Crystalline vs Amorphous – Comparison

S.NoCrystallineAmorphous
1Regular arrangement of atoms → definite geometric shapeRandom arrangement → no definite geometric shape
2Anisotropic (properties vary with direction)Isotropic (properties same in all directions)
3Sharp melting pointNo sharp melting point (softens over a range)
4Possess internal symmetryDo not possess internal symmetry
Examples
• Metallic crystals: Cu, Ag, Al, W, Mg
• Non-metallic crystals: Ge, Si, Carbon
• Amorphous: Glass, Rubber, Plastic

2. Fundamental Definitions

Space Lattice

A regular and periodic arrangement of an infinite number of points in three-dimensional space. Every point is identical to every other point.

Basis (Motif)

A group of atoms associated with every lattice point.

Crystal

Regular and periodic arrangement of atoms or molecules in three-dimensional space.

\[ \text{Crystal} = \text{Space Lattice} + \text{Basis} \]
Crystal = Lattice + Basis

Single Crystal vs Polycrystalline

Single CrystalPolycrystalline Material
Entire material consists of only one crystal
Examples: KDP, ADP		 Collection of many small crystals separated by well-defined boundaries
Examples: GaP, GaAs, ZnO, ZnS

Fundamental Translation Vector

Mathematical representation of the position vector of any lattice point:

2-D: \( \mathbf{R} = n_1\mathbf{a} + n_2\mathbf{b} \)

3-D: \( \mathbf{R} = n_1\mathbf{a} + n_2\mathbf{b} + n_3\mathbf{c} \)

where \(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c}\) are the fundamental translation vectors and \(n_1,n_2,n_3\) are integers.

Interfacial Angles

Angles between the three crystallographic axes, denoted \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\).

3. Unit Cell & Crystal Structure Parameters

Unit Cell

The smallest geometric volume containing one or more atoms arranged in three dimensions. It is the fundamental building block of a crystal structure.

Primitive Cell

The smallest-volume unit cell that contains lattice points only at the corners.

Lattice Constant

Distance between any two successive lattice points along the crystallographic axes.

Key Parameters

ParameterDefinition
Number of atoms per unit cell (Z) Total number of atoms present in / shared by a unit cell (effective number)
Atomic Radius (r) Half the distance between two nearest-neighbour atoms that are in direct contact. Usually expressed in terms of cube edge \(a\).
Coordination Number (CN) Number of nearest neighbouring atoms surrounding a given atom
Atomic Packing Factor (APF) Ratio of volume occupied by atoms in the unit cell to the total volume of the unit cell
\[ \text{APF} = \frac{u}{v} = \frac{\text{(No. of atoms per cell)} \times \text{(Volume of one atom)}}{\text{Volume of unit cell}} \]

Crystals are grouped into seven systems on the basis of the shape of the unit cell (axial lengths and inter-axial angles).

5. Fourteen Bravais Lattices

In 1848, Auguste Bravais showed that only 14 distinct space lattices are possible. These are the Bravais lattices.

Lattice Centring Types

  • P (Primitive) – lattice points only at the corners
  • I (Body-centred) – corners + body centre
  • F (Face-centred) – corners + centres of all six faces
  • C (Base-centred / End-centred) – corners + centres of top & bottom faces
Cubic system (3 lattices): Primitive (SC), Body-centred (BCC), Face-centred (FCC)
Examples: Au, Cu, Ag, NaCl, diamond

6. Simple Cubic (SC / Primitive) Structure

Eight corner atoms only. Example: Polonium.

Number of Atoms per Unit Cell

Step 1
Each corner atom is shared by 8 unit cells → contribution = \( \frac{1}{8} \)
Step 2
Total atoms = \( 8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1 \)
\[ Z_{\text{SC}} = 1 \]

Atomic Radius

Corner atoms touch along the edge:

\[ a = 2r \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = \frac{a}{2} \]

Coordination Number

Each atom has 6 nearest neighbours (4 in its plane + 1 above + 1 below).

\[ \text{CN}_{\text{SC}} = 6 \]

Packing Factor – Full Derivation

Volume of atoms in cell
\( u = Z \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 1 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi \left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi \cdot \frac{a^3}{8} = \frac{\pi a^3}{6} \)
Volume of unit cell
\( v = a^3 \)
APF
\( \text{APF} = \frac{u}{v} = \frac{\pi a^3 / 6}{a^3} = \frac{\pi}{6} \approx 0.52 \)
\[ \text{APF}_{\text{SC}} = 0.52 \quad (52\% \text{ occupied, } 48\% \text{ vacant}) \]
SC is a loosely packed structure.

7. Body-Centred Cubic (BCC) Structure

Eight corner atoms + one atom at the body centre. Examples: W, Cr, Mo, α-Fe.

Number of Atoms per Unit Cell

Corner contribution: \( 8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1 \)
Body-centre contribution: \( 1 \times 1 = 1 \)
Total: \( Z_{\text{BCC}} = 2 \)

Atomic Radius – Derivation

Atoms touch along the body diagonal.

Body diagonal length
\( AD = \sqrt{a^2 + a^2 + a^2} = a\sqrt{3} \)
In terms of radius
Body diagonal = \( 4r \) (two corner radii + two radii of centre atom)
Equate
\( 4r = a\sqrt{3} \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = \frac{a\sqrt{3}}{4} \)
\[ r_{\text{BCC}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}\, a \]

Coordination Number

The body-centre atom is touched by all 8 corner atoms.

\[ \text{CN}_{\text{BCC}} = 8 \]

Packing Factor – Full Derivation

\( u = 2 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 2 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi \left(\frac{a\sqrt{3}}{4}\right)^3 = \frac{8\pi}{3} \cdot \frac{3\sqrt{3}\, a^3}{64} = \frac{\pi a^3 \sqrt{3}}{8} \)
\( v = a^3 \)
\( \text{APF} = \frac{\pi\sqrt{3}}{8} \approx 0.68 \)
\[ \text{APF}_{\text{BCC}} = 0.68 \quad (68\% \text{ occupied}) \]

8. Face-Centred Cubic (FCC) Structure

Eight corner atoms + six face-centred atoms. Examples: Cu, Ag, Au, Al, Pb, Ni.

Number of Atoms per Unit Cell

Corner contribution: \( 8 \times \frac{1}{8} = 1 \)
Face-centre contribution: \( 6 \times \frac{1}{2} = 3 \)
Total: \( Z_{\text{FCC}} = 4 \)

Atomic Radius – Derivation

Atoms touch along the face diagonal.

Face diagonal
\( AC = \sqrt{a^2 + a^2} = a\sqrt{2} \)
In terms of radius
Face diagonal = \( 4r \)
\( 4r = a\sqrt{2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad r = \frac{a\sqrt{2}}{4} = \frac{a}{2\sqrt{2}} \)
\[ r_{\text{FCC}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{4}\, a \]

Coordination Number

Each atom has 12 nearest neighbours (4 in its plane + 4 above + 4 below).

\[ \text{CN}_{\text{FCC}} = 12 \]

Packing Factor – Full Derivation

\( u = 4 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 = 4 \times \frac{4}{3}\pi \left(\frac{a\sqrt{2}}{4}\right)^3 = \frac{16\pi}{3} \cdot \frac{2\sqrt{2}\, a^3}{64} = \frac{\pi a^3 \sqrt{2}}{6} \)
\( v = a^3 \)
\( \text{APF} = \frac{\pi\sqrt{2}}{6} \approx 0.74 \)
\[ \text{APF}_{\text{FCC}} = 0.74 \quad (74\% \text{ occupied}) \]
FCC is the most closely packed of the three cubic structures.

10. Miller Indices

Miller indices are a symbolic vector representation for the orientation of an atomic plane (or direction) in a crystal lattice. They are defined as the reciprocals of the fractional intercepts that the plane makes with the crystallographic axes.

Procedure to Find Miller Indices of a Plane

  1. Find the intercepts of the plane on the X, Y and Z axes (in units of a, b, c).
  2. Take the reciprocals of these intercepts.
  3. Clear fractions by multiplying by the LCM of the denominators → obtain three integers h, k, l.
  4. Write the integers in parentheses: (h k l).
Example
Intercepts = 1, 3, 2
Reciprocals = \( 1,\; \frac{1}{3},\; \frac{1}{2} \)
Multiply by 6 → 6, 2, 3
Miller indices = (6 2 3)

Important Features

  • All equally spaced parallel planes have the same Miller indices.
  • Miller indices define a family of parallel planes, not a single plane.
  • If a plane is parallel to an axis, its intercept is ∞ → corresponding index = 0.
  • Negative intercept → bar over the index, e.g. \( (\bar{1}11) \).
  • Written without commas: (100), (110), (111), etc.
Common low-index planes in a cubic crystal

11. Interplanar Distance (d-spacing)

The perpendicular distance between two adjacent parallel planes with Miller indices (hkl) is called the interplanar spacing \( d_{hkl} \).

For a cubic crystal: \[ d_{hkl} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + l^2}} \]
As the Miller indices increase, the interplanar spacing decreases.
Example: \( d_{100} = a \), \( d_{110} = a/\sqrt{2} \), \( d_{111} = a/\sqrt{3} \)

12. Crystal Imperfections (Defects)

In an ideal crystal the atomic arrangement is perfectly regular. In real crystals the regularity is disturbed. These disturbances are called crystal defects or imperfections.

Structure-sensitive properties (affected by defects): mechanical strength, ductility, crystal growth, magnetic hysteresis, dielectric strength.
Structure-insensitive properties (not affected): stiffness, density.

Classification by Geometry

DimensionTypeExamples
0-D (Point)Point defectsVacancies, Interstitials, Impurities
1-D (Line)Line defectsEdge dislocation, Screw dislocation
2-D (Surface)Surface defectsGrain boundaries, Twin boundaries, Stacking faults
3-D (Volume)Volume defectsCracks, voids, precipitates

13. Point Defects

Imperfections of atomic dimensions (size ≈ 1–2 atomic diameters).

(a) Vacancy

An atom is missing from a normally occupied lattice site. Neighbouring atoms are displaced inward.

Schottky Defect

Pair of vacancies – one cation and one anion missing in an ionic crystal (maintains charge neutrality).

Frenkel Defect

An atom (usually cation) leaves its lattice site and occupies an interstitial position → vacancy + interstitial pair.

(b) Interstitial Defect

  • Self-interstitial – atom of the same crystal occupies an interstitial site.
  • Foreign interstitial – impurity atom occupies an interstitial site.

(c) Impurity Defects

  • Substitutional impurity – foreign atom replaces a host atom (e.g., Zn in Cu for brass; P or B in Si for n/p-type semiconductors).
  • Interstitial impurity – small foreign atom sits in an interstitial void (e.g., C in FCC iron).

14. Line Defects (Dislocations)

Distortion of atoms along a line. Two main types:

(a) Edge Dislocation (Taylor–Orowan dislocation)

An extra half-plane of atoms is inserted into the crystal. The edge of this half-plane is the dislocation line.

  • Positive edge dislocation (⊥) – extra plane above the slip plane.
  • Negative edge dislocation (⊤) – extra plane below the slip plane.

(b) Screw Dislocation (Burgers dislocation)

The crystal planes are warped into a spiral ramp around the dislocation line, resembling a screw thread.

Burgers Vector

  • Characterises the magnitude and direction of the lattice distortion.
  • For an edge dislocation, Burgers vector is perpendicular to the dislocation line.
  • For a screw dislocation, Burgers vector is parallel (or anti-parallel) to the dislocation line.
  • Energy of a dislocation ∝ \( |\mathbf{b}|^2 \).

16. Bridgman Technique

Named after Bridgman (1925) and Stockbarger (1938). A method of growing single-crystal ingots (boules) from the melt.

Principle
Polycrystalline material is heated above its melting point in a crucible. The crucible is slowly lowered (or the furnace is moved) so that solidification starts from one end (often seeded) and a single crystal grows progressively along the length of the container. Can be performed in vertical or horizontal geometry.
Schematic of vertical Bridgman furnace

Advantages

  • Technically simple
  • Low cost
  • Crystal diameter controlled by crucible size

Disadvantage

  • Container contraction on cooling can introduce stresses → dislocations

17. Slow Evaporation Method

A solution-growth technique widely used for organic and inorganic crystals at low temperature.

Principle
A saturated (or near-saturated) solution is kept at constant temperature. Solvent is allowed to evaporate slowly. As the solution becomes supersaturated, solute crystallises out.

Key Principles

  1. Controlled evaporation – slow rate gives time for ordered arrangement of solute molecules.
  2. Solute saturation – solution should be near saturation so that crystallisation begins as soon as a little solvent is lost.
  3. Solvent choice – good solvent for the solute; evaporates at a controllable rate (water, ethanol, acetone, etc.).

Typical conditions: temperature stability ≈ 0.05 °C, evaporation rate of a few mm³ h⁻¹.

Advantages

  • High precision & well-defined morphology
  • Good reproducibility
  • Minimised impurity incorporation
  • Scalable

Disadvantages

  • Solute must not react with solvent
  • Only for fairly soluble substances
  • Tiny crystals on vessel walls can fall and act as secondary nuclei
  • Variable evaporation rate affects quality
